# Public-domain sources for jazz "language" (etudes, lines, choruses): what is PD under US law

*Research done 2026-11-22. **This is research to support a product decision, not legal advice.** The owner's rule is to use only public-domain sources, judged by **US law as of November 2026**. Anything that will ship in the paid product should be checked by a US copyright lawyer, especially §3 (recorded improvisations).*

*Tags: **[V]** = checked against the linked source during this research. **[?]** = not verified, inferred, or legally unsettled. Related files: `docs/research/STANDARDS_LEGAL.md` (which tunes and lead sheets we can use) and `docs/research/MUSIC_SOURCES_LEGAL.md` (general sources).*

## 0. Bottom line

| Source of "language" | PD in the US now? | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Any musical composition **published (or registered unpublished) in 1930 or earlier**, in its original period edition | **Yes** | High [V] |
| Published US compositions from 1931–1963 that were **not renewed** | Yes, but you have to prove non-renewal, and foreign works were restored | Medium. Searches are slow and never conclusive [V] |
| **1920s published "hot chorus" and "breaks" folios** (Armstrong *50 Hot Choruses* / *125 Jazz Breaks for Cornet*, Melrose 1927; similar Robbins/Melrose folios) | **Yes**, in the original printing | High on the law; the exact year (1927 or 1928) is [?], but both are ≤1930 |
| Published ragtime and stride piano pieces ≤1930 (Joplin, Morton, J. P. Johnson, Confrey, Bix *In a Mist*) and 19th-century etudes (Czerny, Hanon) | **Yes**, in original editions | High [V] on the rule; per-title dates [?] |
| **Sound recordings** published ≤1925 | **Yes**. The audio itself is free | High [V] |
| **Improvised solos that exist only on records** (never published as sheet music) | **Depends on when the soloist died**, not on how old the record is. These are probably *unpublished* works under §303(b): life + 70, and never earlier than the end of 2002 | Law fairly clear; the application has open risks [?] (see §3) |
| → soloist died **1955 or earlier** (e.g. Bix, King Oliver, Morton, Waller, Charlie Christian, Blanton, **Charlie Parker**) | **Probably PD**, unless an authorized publication between 1978 and 2002 pushed the term to 2047, or a label has a work-for-hire claim | [?], needs lawyer and per-solo checks |
| → soloist died after 1955 (e.g. **Armstrong, d. 1971 → through 2041**; Hawkins; Lester Young) | **No**, even if the 1925 record is PD | Medium-high [?] |
| Chord progressions; short generic cells (enclosures, 4–8-note bebop cells, scales, arpeggios) | **Not protectable** on their own | High for scales, arpeggios and progressions [V]; "a specific 8-note cell" is high but not absolute [V/?] |

**Practical recommendation:** build the etude library from (a) published ≤1930 folios and pieces, scanned from **original printings**, (b) lines written by a human over PD changes (or any changes, since progressions are free), using only generic vocabulary, and (c) optionally, transcriptions of solos by players who died ≤1955, after a lawyer signs off on the §3 analysis. **Never** transcribe Armstrong, Hawkins, Young, Gillespie and the like from records, and never copy from modern transcription or etude books.

## 1. Published musical compositions in 2026

### 1.1 The cutoff: published 1930 or earlier = PD
- The Copyright Office says the maximum term for works under the 1909 Act is 95 years (28-year first term plus a 67-year renewal), and "all works published in the United States before January 1, 1931, are in the public domain" — Circular 15A, *Duration of Copyright*, https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ15a.pdf **[V]** (the statute is 17 U.S.C. §304, https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/304).
- Duke CSPD, *Public Domain Day 2026*: works first published or registered in **1930** entered the US public domain on 1 Jan 2026. The 1930 compositions it names include *I Got Rhythm*, *Body and Soul*, *Embraceable You*, *But Not for Me*, *Georgia on My Mind* and *On the Sunny Side of the Street* — https://cspd.law.duke.edu/publicdomainday/2026/ **[V]**
- **Registration counts like publication.** Under the 1909 Act, copyright could be secured by registering an unpublished work. Duke treats a work registered in 1930 but published in 1931 (*Dream a Little Dream of Me*) as PD in 2026, and *Mood Indigo* (recorded 1930, sheet music 1931) as PD only in 2027 (same page) **[V]**. The relevant date is the **earlier of publication or registration**, never the recording date.
- The Cornell chart (Hirtle), https://guides.library.cornell.edu/copyright/publicdomain, starts its published-works table at 1931, which means everything earlier is PD **[V]**.

### 1.2 Works from 1931–1963: the renewal rule
- Works first published or registered 1931–1963 lasted **28 years only, unless renewed in the 28th year**. If renewed, the term is 95 years from publication. Works from 1964–1977 were renewed automatically by the Copyright Renewal Act of 1992 (P.L. 102-307) — Circular 15A, and Circular 22 *How to Investigate the Copyright Status of a Work*, https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ22.pdf **[V]**. The Cornell chart lists 1931–1963 published with notice and not renewed as PD, and 1931–1963 renewed as 95 years **[V]**.
- Works published 1931–1977 **without a notice** are PD (Cornell; Circular 22) **[V]**. For a printed 1930s–60s jazz folio, check the original for a © line.
- Cornell's footnote cites studies finding that fewer than 15% of registered works were renewed (7% of books) **[V]**. Music was renewed more often than books, though, because publishers valued catalogs **[?]**. So the "most things weren't renewed" heuristic is weaker for popular music.

**How to check non-renewal (US works only):**
1. Find the **original registration**: the year, claimant, and whether it was published or unpublished (unpublished = "Eu" numbers, published = "Ep"). Source: the *Catalog of Copyright Entries* (CCE), Part 3 "Musical Compositions", digitized at https://archive.org/details/copyrightrecords (link given in Circular 22) **[V]**.
2. Search for a **renewal in the 28th year** (and the year before or after, since filing could start at the end of year 27):
   - Renewals filed **1978 or later** (originals from 1950–1963) are in the Copyright Office online catalog / Copyright Public Records System, which covers 1978 to now (Circular 22) **[V]**.
   - Renewals filed **before 1978** (originals 1931–1949) appear only in the printed CCE: "renewals… are generally listed at the end of the volume containing the class of work". Renewals from 1979–1982 are in CCE Section 8 (Circular 22) **[V]**.
   - The Copyright Office's Virtual Card Catalog (1870–1977 cards) is a further source **[?]** (it exists; we haven't tested how complete it is for music).
3. Search every title variant and every claimant (publisher and author), because renewals were often filed by heirs.
4. Keep the evidence (screenshots and CCE page references) in a rights file per item.
- Circular 22 itself warns that even a full search "may not be conclusive" **[V]**.
- **Foreign works are an exception.** Under 17 U.S.C. §104A (URAA restoration), works by foreign authors first published abroad had their US copyright restored without regard to notice or renewal. Duke notes the restored foreign works, for which the only question is the publication year (Duke 2026 page) **[V]**. So for a European jazz folio from 1931–1963 (e.g. by Django Reinhardt's publishers), **non-renewal doesn't help**. Treat it as protected for 95 years **[V on the principle; §104A at https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/104A]**.

**Recommendation:** for v1, use **≤1930 material only**. Use 1931–1963 non-renewed US material only case by case, with a documented search.

### 1.3 New editions and arrangements have their own copyright
- A new arrangement, edition or "editorial revision" of a PD work is a **derivative work**, and its *new* material can be protected. The Compendium says musical editing such as "additional or altered fingering, accents, dynamics, and the like" can be registered, but only as to the new material — *Compendium (Third)* §802.6(I), https://www.copyright.gov/comp3/chap800/ch800-performing-arts.pdf **[V]**; 17 U.S.C. §103(b) **[V principle]**.
- Example: a 2010 arrangement of Bix's *In a Mist* is sold with a current notice held by EMI Robbins/Alfred (ejazzlines listing, found by search) **[V that a modern edition is claimed]**.
- **Rule for us:** work only from **original period printings** (Library of Congress, the Levy Collection, IMSLP scans of the first edition, archive.org), and enter the notes ourselves. Don't copy modern "urtext" fingerings, Hal Leonard reprints or fake-book layouts.
- Small plus: the Compendium says that "transcription" in the sense of notating music someone else created gives the transcriber nothing registrable — §802.9(E)(6) **[V]**. So a modern transcriber of a PD piece or solo has no copyright in the *notes*. They could still claim layout and compilation, and possibly editorial additions **[?]**. We still re-transcribe independently, to avoid any argument.

## 2. Sound recordings: the Music Modernization Act schedule

Pre-1972 recordings were brought under federal law by Title II of the MMA (the "CLASSICS Act"), 17 U.S.C. §1401 (https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/1401). The Copyright Office summary is at https://www.copyright.gov/music-modernization/pre1972-soundrecordings/ **[V]**:

| First published | Protected through | PD in 2026? |
|---|---|---|
| Before 1923 | 31 Dec 2021 | Yes [V] |
| 1923–1946 | 95 years + 5 = **31 Dec of (year + 100)** | **Recordings from 1925 and earlier are PD** (1925 ones since 1 Jan 2026) [V] |
| 1947–1956 | 95 + 15 = 31 Dec of (year + 110) | No [V] |
| 1957 – 14 Feb 1972 (and unpublished pre-1972 recordings) | 15 Feb 2067 | No [V] |
| 15 Feb 1972 onward | Normal federal term | No |

- Duke 2026: **1925 recordings** became PD on 1 Jan 2026. Examples include Bessie Smith's *St. Louis Blues* with Louis Armstrong and Clarence Williams' Blue Five *Everybody Loves My Baby* (with Armstrong) **[V]**. Duke 2025 lists 1924 recordings such as King Oliver's *Krooked Blues* (with Armstrong) and Morton's *Shreveport Stomp* **[V]**. The Cornell chart agrees: "before 1926: PD" **[V]**.
- §1401 covers the **sound recording only**. Nothing in it touches the underlying musical work (reading of the statute; Copyright Office page) **[V]**. So the first Hot Five sides (Nov 1925) are free **as audio**, but the 1926–1928 Hot Five and Hot Seven sides stay protected until 2027–2029.
- Duke also notes that imitating a recording is allowed but copying the recording is not (2026 page) **[V]**. A sound-recording copyright does not stop someone from writing down the notes. What stops them is the *musical-work* copyright, if there is one (see §3).

## 3. The key nuance: an improvised solo that exists only on a pre-1978 record

### 3.1 The rule
- **17 U.S.C. §303(b):** distributing phonorecords before 1 Jan 1978 "shall not for any purpose constitute a publication of any musical work, dramatic work, or literary work embodied therein" — https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/303 **[V]**. Pub. L. 105-80 (1997) added it, and Pub. L. 111-295 (2010) widened it from "the musical work" to "any musical, dramatic, or literary work" (GovInfo notes to §303, https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2012-title17/html/USCODE-2012-title17-chap3.htm) **[V]**.
- **Why it was added:** *La Cienega Music Co. v. ZZ Top*, 53 F.3d 950 (9th Cir. 1995), held that "selling recordings constitutes 'publication'" under the 1909 Act. The consequence: a song released on record without notice and registration fell into the public domain. This split from the Second Circuit's *Rosette v. Rainbo Record Mfg.* (S.D.N.Y. 1973, aff'd 2d Cir.) — summary at GWU Music Copyright Infringement Resource, https://blogs.law.gwu.edu/mcir/?p=6034 **[V]**. Congress reversed *La Cienega* with §303(b). The Besek study for the Library of Congress/CLIR (pub. 135, §2.2.3) describes the 1997 amendment and says that sheet-music distribution, by contrast, would be publication — https://www.clir.org/pubs/reports/pub135/sec2/ **[V]**.
- *ABKCO Music v. LaVere*, 217 F.3d 684 (9th Cir. 2000), the Robert Johnson *Love in Vain* / *Stop Breakin' Down* case: asked whether late-1930s record releases "published" the compositions, and applied the 1997 amendment so that they did not **[V that the case exists and frames this question (Leagle listing); [?] on the exact reasoning. We could not open the full opinion.]**
- **Term for works unpublished on 1 Jan 1978 (§303(a)):** the normal §302 term applies: **life of the author + 70 years**. For works made for hire or anonymous works it is the *shorter* of 95 years from publication or 120 years from creation. It can never expire before **31 Dec 2002**. If the work was published on or before 31 Dec 2002, it can't expire before **31 Dec 2047** — Circular 15A; §303(a) **[V]**. The Cornell chart's 2026 line reads "unpublished works whose authors died before 1956: PD" **[V]**.
- **An improvised solo is a "work" once it is fixed.** The Compendium says improvised works are registrable when "fixed in tangible form, such as in a transcribed copy, a phonorecord, or an audiovisual recording" — §802.4 **[V]**. Under 17 U.S.C. §101, a work is "created" when first fixed in a copy *or phonorecord* **[V principle]**.

### 3.2 Applied to a 1925 Armstrong solo
Here is the analysis. Each step rests on the sources above, but **no court or Copyright Office document we found applies it to a jazz solo specifically [?]**:
1. Armstrong's improvised chorus is original musical expression, fixed in 1925 on an OKeh record.
2. If it was never published in notation and never registered, it was **unpublished**. Selling the 78 did not publish it (§303(b)). Before 1978 it had state common-law protection. On 1 Jan 1978 it moved to federal protection under §303(a).
3. Term: Armstrong died in 1971, so life + 70 runs **through 31 Dec 2041** (more than the 2002 floor). The **1925 recording is PD (§2), but the notes of the solo are probably not.** Transcribing and distributing it in an app would reproduce an unpublished musical work.
4. If the solo is played over someone else's then-copyrighted tune, it is also a derivative work. §103(a) denies protection to parts that use preexisting material unlawfully **[?]** on how that applies to a licensed 1920s recording. The improvised notes are still the improviser's.

**Things that could change the answer for a given solo [?]:**
- **It was published in notation at the time.** The 1927 Melrose folios published Armstrong-style choruses and breaks (§4.2). Anything printed with notice ≤1930 is now PD. *But the folio choruses are not the same notes as the recorded solos.* Only what is printed is free.
- **The head was registered as a composition** (e.g. Duke lists *Potato Head Blues* and *Gully Low Blues*, 1927, as PD compositions, https://cspd.law.duke.edu/publicdomainday/2023/) **[V]**. Under *Skidmore v. Led Zeppelin*, 952 F.3d 1051 (9th Cir. 2020) (en banc), and *Structured Asset Sales v. Sheeran*, 120 F.4th 1066 (2d Cir. 2024), a 1909-Act composition copyright covers **only the deposit copy** **[V]** (Justia summaries: https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca9/16-56057/16-56057-2020-03-09.html , https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca2/23-905/23-905-2024-11-01.html). So the PD composition = the deposited lead sheet. A recorded solo that isn't in the deposit is **not** freed by the composition's PD status. It is then either a separate unpublished work (most likely) or unprotected; this is unsettled **[?]**.
- **Work made for hire.** Under the 1909 Act's "instance and expense" test, a label or bandleader might claim it owns the solo as an employer. For an unpublished work for hire the term is 120 years from creation, so a 1925 solo would run to the end of 2045 **[?]**. This only matters if the improviser died ≤1955, because otherwise life + 70 protects it anyway.
- **Authorized publication 1978–2002 extends the term to 2047 (§303(a))** **[V on the rule]**. Example risk: posthumous transcription books such as the *Charlie Parker Omnibook* (first published 1978, © Atlantic Music Corp.) **[?]**. If that was an authorized publication by the owner of Parker's unpublished solos, the solos it contains may be protected through 2047, not PD since 2026. An unauthorized publication doesn't count as publication. Each solo needs this check.
- **Joint authorship** with the rhythm section or other soloists is a theoretical complication; it doesn't change the term unless a co-author lived longer **[?]**.

### 3.3 Practical conclusion
- **The age of the record doesn't matter. The improviser's death year does.**
  - Improviser died **after 1955** (Armstrong 1971 → 2041; Coleman Hawkins 1969 → 2039; Lester Young 1959 → 2029; Gillespie, Getz and others much later): **don't transcribe their recorded solos.** Treat them as protected. *[death years from standard references, not re-verified ↔ [?]]*
  - Improviser died **1955 or earlier**: candidates include Bix Beiderbecke (1931), Bubber Miley (1932), King Oliver (1938), Johnny Dodds (1940), Jelly Roll Morton (1941), Chu Berry (1941), Charlie Christian (1942), Jimmy Blanton (1942), Bunny Berigan (1942), Fats Waller (1943), Jimmie Noone (1944), Fats Navarro (1950), Django Reinhardt (1953) and **Charlie Parker (Mar 1955)**. Their unpublished recorded solos are **probably PD in the US as of 2026**. That holds **only if** (a) no authorized publication 1978–2002 (→ 2047) and (b) no credible work-for-hire claim (→ 120 years from creation, i.e. a solo recorded 1906–1925 is PD by 2045 at the latest, and later ones are not) **[?, death years [?]]**. Clifford Brown and Art Tatum (both d. 1956) join on 1 Jan 2027 **[?]**.
- Even when the solo is PD, **the record usually is not** (anything after 1925). We may *listen* to transcribe, but we can't ship the audio. Our band re-plays the line.
- Transcribe **ourselves** from the record. Don't copy a published transcription; its notes may be uncopyrightable (Compendium §802.9(E)(6)), but its editing and compilation may not be.
- **Lowest-risk path for v1:** don't depend on §3 at all. Use §4 sources plus human-written lines. Add a pre-1956-death "classic solos" pack only after a US lawyer confirms §3.2 and the per-solo checks are done.

## 5. Are short generic vocabulary devices protectable?

**Short answer: no, not on their own.** Scales, arpeggios, enclosures, chromatic approach tones, standard ii–V cells and similar building blocks are not protectable. A *longer specific line* can be, just like a melody.

- **Copyright Office:** "Diatonic or chromatic scales", "Arpeggios" and "Chord symbols based on standard chord progressions" are common property, and "short musical phrases are not copyrightable because they lack a sufficient amount of authorship". A few notes like a clock chime are "too short". There is "no predetermined number of notes" that decides it — *Compendium (Third)* §802.5(A)–(B), citing 37 C.F.R. §202.1(a) (words and short phrases) **[V]**. Method books built from scales, arpeggios and exercises are protected only for their original text, new music or compilation (§802.6(J)) **[V]**.
- ***Gray v. Hudson***, 28 F.4th 87 (9th Cir. 2022) (Katy Perry, *Dark Horse*): the court set aside a jury verdict and held the evidence legally insufficient to show that an **8-note ostinato** was protectable original expression. The pattern was built from commonplace elements: a minor scale, even rhythm, repetition — https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca9/20-55401/20-55401-2022-03-10.html **[V on outcome; detailed reasoning [?]]**. This is the closest analogue to "a 4–8-note bebop cell".
- ***Skidmore v. Led Zeppelin*** (9th Cir. 2020, en banc): upheld a jury instruction that common musical elements are not protected. The instruction is widely reported as listing descending chromatic scales, arpeggios and short sequences of three notes **[V on the case; that exact wording [?]]**. It also confirmed that an original *selection and arrangement* of unprotectable elements can be protected **[V via summaries]**.
- ***Newton v. Diamond***, 388 F.3d 1189 (9th Cir. 2004): copying a three-note figure (C–D♭–C over a held C) from a composition was **de minimis** and not infringing; the district court had also found it unoriginal — https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/388/1189/569849 **[V on outcome]**.
- ***Structured Asset Sales v. Sheeran*** (2d Cir. 2024): a common chord progression plus harmonic rhythm is unprotectable; a selection-and-arrangement claim built on them fails **[V]**. Commentators describe this as scènes à faire / building blocks; the opinion's exact label is [?].

**Practical rule for our etude writers:**
1. Generic devices are free: enclosures, the "bebop scale" passing tone, 1-2-3-5 cells, digital patterns, chromatic approaches, Barry Harris-style scale rules, guide-tone lines.
2. **Don't reproduce a recognizable *specific* phrase** from a protected solo or etude book (e.g. a distinctive 2-bar Parker lick as he played it, or a Bergonzi pattern sequence as printed). The more notes, rhythm and contour that match, the riskier. Note that the protected sequence would usually be longer than one cell, and in Parker's case his unpublished solos are probably PD anyway (§3).
3. A full 32-bar etude that strings generic cells together is *our* original work. It is protected for us, not a copy of anyone else's.

## 6. Checklist per item before it ships
1. Is it a **composition published or registered ≤1930**? Keep a scan of the © line from the **original printing**.
2. If 1931–1963: it's a US work, and a CCE/online catalog search shows no renewal. Save the evidence. Foreign works are excluded.
3. Notes entered **by us** from the original, with no modern editorial additions copied.
4. If it is a **recorded solo**: the improviser died ≤1955 (death date from a reliable source); no authorized notation published 1978–2002; no label work-for-hire claim known; a lawyer has signed off on §3.
5. Audio: use only recordings ≤1925, or our own rendering.
6. Log the source and reasoning in a per-item rights record. Release this as a **US-only** decision; other countries need their own check (see `STANDARDS_LEGAL.md`).

## Summary
- **Compositions:** everything published or registered **≤1930** is PD in the US (2026). 1931–1963 US works are PD only if not renewed, which takes a CCE/online-catalog search that is never conclusive. Foreign works were restored. Modern editions and arrangements carry their own copyright, so work from original printings.
- **Recordings:** only those published **≤1925** are PD (MMA: 1923–46 = 100 years). The first Hot Five sides are free as audio; later ones are not.
- **Recorded improvisations (the key point):** under §303(b), a pre-1978 record did not publish the solo, so it is an **unpublished work: life + 70 (minimum end of 2002; 2047 if authorized publication happened 1978–2002)**. The improviser's death year governs, not the record's age. **Armstrong's solos are protected through 2041 even though the 1925 records are PD.** Solos by players who died ≤1955 (Bix, Oliver, Morton, Waller, Christian, Blanton, Parker…) are *probably* PD now, subject to per-solo checks (no authorized 1978–2002 publication, no work-for-hire claim) and a lawyer's sign-off.
- **Best PD "etude" sources:** Armstrong's *50 Hot Choruses* and *125 Jazz Breaks* (Melrose, 1927/28) and similar 1920s breaks folios; ≤1930 ragtime and stride pieces and *In a Mist*; 19th-century etudes (Arban, Czerny, Hanon); plus our own lines over any changes.
- **Progressions and short cells** (scales, arpeggios, enclosures, short bebop cells) are not protectable (Compendium §802.5; *Gray*, *Skidmore*, *Newton*, *Sheeran*). Avoid copying recognizable longer phrases from protected solos and books.
